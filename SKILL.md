# Similar

Turn a discovery or topic into connected opportunities the user can actually use.

## Start from the benefit

Accept either a discovery or a broad topic:

- **Discovery:** Identify the benefit, what made it work, and relevant constraints.
- **Topic:** Start with a brief, useful exploration of its main mechanisms and related
  opportunities. State a necessary scope assumption in one sentence. Do not demand
  an anecdote, invent a personal goal, or turn the answer into just a glossary.

An explicit topic takes priority over unrelated earlier conversation. Use that earlier
context only when the user connects it to this request. If neither a topic nor a
discovery is available, ask for one; otherwise ask only when the answer would materially
change the work. Treat anecdotes as leads and correct mistaken premises. If only an
explanation or a related alternative is supportable, label it clearly rather than
silently substituting a different topic or objective.

## Two levels, three approaches at each

Work through both levels internally unless the user narrows the request:

1. **Same benefit:** Identify the example's base function and find materially different
   ways to achieve that outcome. Search beyond its exact object, defect, or wording.
2. **Same principle, elsewhere:** Identify the strongest underlying mechanisms and
   transfer them to other areas of life. Consider multiple plausible themes before
   selecting the ones that yield concrete opportunities. Avoid abstractions so broad
   that any generic life tip would qualify. Every candidate must have a defensible
   connection to the starting discovery and a tangible benefit.

The second level must leave the starting domain unless the user explicitly narrows
it. Adjacent tactics within the same domain do not count: for an investing example,
other assets, investment taxes, and brokerage features still belong in the first
group. Search other areas such as travel, shopping, work, or building products only
where a specific mechanism transfers. "Both save or make money" is not enough.
Explain that connection in ordinary language when it is not obvious.

At each level, use all three approaches:

- **Straightforward:** Find the strongest conventional routes.
- **Outside the box:** Challenge assumptions, change the mechanism, or borrow an
  approach from another domain. These opportunities may be widely accessible but
  easily overlooked.
- **Special circumstances:** Find routes enabled by unusual resources, access,
  capabilities, memberships, scale, location, or other advantages, even when it is
  unknown whether the user has them. Identify the actual gate and a realistic way
  to qualify when one exists. Do not assume eligibility.

Explore broadly across mechanisms and domains, then judge across all three approaches.
Prioritize useful benefit, evidence, fit, effort, costs, and realistic access. Remove
duplicates and superficial variations. Do not force equal representation, a fixed
number of findings, or exactly one item per approach. Stop when further searching
mostly repeats existing mechanisms or adds weaker results; never claim to have
exhausted all possibilities.

## Make opportunities real

Verify current, local, obscure, or consequential claims, especially policies,
entitlements, deadlines, costs, and eligibility. Distinguish a published entitlement
from a discretionary concession or anecdotal success. Do not turn the user's reported
success rate into a prediction. Prefer original sources for deciding facts. Put
material conditions next to the relevant idea.

For a policy-based opportunity, identify the actual provider, jurisdiction, or rule
when relevant, link the supporting source, and make the user's next move concrete.
Mark a promising unverified route as a lead rather than presenting it as established.
Do not invent exact benefits, refunds, loopholes, or approval odds.

Seek legitimate advantages grounded in real circumstances. Do not propose fabricated
defects, altered labels, false claims, or concealment to obtain benefits. Keep this
check internal unless it materially affects a finding. Research does not authorize
submitting claims, sending messages, making purchases, or changing accounts.

If live research is unavailable, use supplied evidence and stable reasoning; label
unverified possibilities as leads and say what needs checking. Never invent sources.

## Use the current assistant

Carry out the research and synthesis with the current assistant and its available
tools. This skill does not delegate research to another assistant or service.

## Chat output: easy-to-understand opportunity cards

Research thoroughly, then explain the strongest useful findings in everyday language.
The reader should understand what happens and why it helps on the first read, without
knowing the field. Shortening jargon alone does not make an explanation accessible.

Use two groups with short, familiar bold labels tailored to the example. Number
cards within each group starting at 1, strongest first, with a horizontal rule between
cards. Select findings for usefulness and distinctness, not to fill a quota. Keep
weaker candidates and detailed research out of the initial answer. If a level has no
solid findings, say so briefly. Follow the user's requested format when different.
Do not put cards in code fences or writing blocks. Use native cards if the surface
supports them; ordinary Markdown is enough. Avoid tables unless a comparison is
clearly easier to read that way.

When simplifying or reformatting an existing answer, preserve every distinct finding
unless the user asks for a shortlist or a correction makes a finding invalid. Simplify
the wording and explanation, not the coverage. Do not silently drop ideas to make the
answer shorter. If a finding is withdrawn, identify it and briefly explain why.

For each card:

- Use a short title describing the action or benefit in ordinary words. Explain the
  idea before introducing any technical name; omit that name if it adds nothing.
- Explain who does what and what the user gains in a few short sentences. Use a tiny
  concrete example when the mechanism would otherwise be abstract. Clearly label
  invented numbers as hypothetical, never as a current offer or expected return.
- Include the main catch or eligibility condition beside the benefit. Clearly say
  whether it is available now, a historical example, or an unverified lead. Keep any
  extra conditions that materially change the decision; do not bury them for brevity.
- Give one practical next move, in ordinary words. Integrate it naturally rather
  than repeating a full Benefit / Conditions / Next step template on every card.
- Keep supporting links beside the claims, using short descriptive labels. Put
  detailed study statistics, methodology, and secondary examples in a deeper answer
  when requested rather than stacking them into the introductory explanation.

For example, explain a financial mechanism as: "Some companies buy shares back and
let small shareholders go first. Hypothetically, buying 99 shares for $48 and selling
them to the company for $50 would leave $198 before fees, if all are accepted and the
deal completes. The special treatment only applies to certain offers, and we have
not found an open profitable one yet." Do not lead with "odd-lot priority and
proration". Adapt this approach to the actual domain; it is not a finance-only format.

Start with the useful findings or one brief sentence identifying the best place to
start. Keep the research framework and internal reasoning out of the answer.
Briefly disclose material research limits when they affect the findings.

Before presenting the run as complete, check that the second group contains
source-backed opportunities outside the original domain, or clearly states that
none were established after searching. Do not relabel same-domain ideas as the
missing group. If the user flags this omission, acknowledge it and complete the
missing research rather than only changing the instructions for future runs.

Before sending, read the answer as a newcomer: can they explain each idea back in
one sentence, see its practical benefit, and spot the main catch? Replace unexplained
terms, split dense paragraphs, and remove details that do not help that decision.
Keep enough explanation to make sense; do not turn it into cryptic bullet fragments.
Use natural, direct language without em dashes.
