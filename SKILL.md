# Similar

Turn a discovery or topic into connected opportunities the user can actually use.

## Start from the benefit

Accept either a discovery or a broad topic:

- **Discovery:** Identify the benefit, what made it work, and relevant constraints.
- **Topic:** Start with a brief, useful exploration of its main mechanisms and related
  opportunities. State a necessary scope assumption in one sentence. Do not demand
  an anecdote, invent a personal goal, or turn the answer into just a glossary.

An explicit topic takes priority over unrelated earlier conversation. Use that earlier
context only when the user connects it to this request. If neither a topic nor a
discovery is available, ask for one; otherwise ask only when the answer would materially
change the work. Treat anecdotes as leads and correct mistaken premises. If only an
explanation or a related alternative is supportable, label it clearly rather than
silently substituting a different topic or objective.

## Search at two levels

- **Same benefit:** Find materially different ways to achieve the original outcome.
  Go beyond variations of the same object, provider, or wording.
- **Same principle, elsewhere:** Transfer the mechanism to other contexts where it
  creates a tangible benefit. Require a specific causal connection, not merely a
  shared theme such as "saving money."

At each level, consider conventional routes, inventive alternatives, and routes
enabled by special circumstances such as access, expertise, location, or assets.
These are research lenses, not quotas or required output sections. Unknown access
does not rule out a route: explain the condition without assuming eligibility.
Respect an explicitly narrowed scope. Remove duplicates and weak analogies; stop when
additional exploration mostly repeats existing mechanisms or adds weaker findings.

## Make the opportunities real

Use available research tools to verify current or consequential claims, especially
eligibility, costs, deadlines, policies, and provider-specific benefits. Prefer
original sources for deciding facts. Distinguish documented rules, discretionary
offers, anecdotes, and untested ideas. Model agreement is not verification.

If live research is unavailable, use supplied evidence and stable reasoning; label
unverified possibilities as leads and say what needs checking. Never invent sources,
entitlements, savings, success rates, or access. Do not promise a fixed number of
results or fill gaps with generic tips. Seek legitimate benefits from real conditions,
without fabricated claims or misrepresentation. Discovery does not authorize spending,
contacting people, or changing accounts.

## One assistant by default

Work with the assistant and tools already available. A second model, account, browser,
or paid service is not required.

If the user requests an independent second pass or thorough mode, use an available,
authorized second researcher. Give it the same starting facts, constraints, and two
search levels without your conclusions. Form your own candidate set before reading
its results, then verify and synthesize. Do not purchase access or send private context
to another service merely to enable this mode. If unavailable, proceed with one
assistant and disclose that independent comparison was not performed.

## Make the answer easy to understand

Write for someone new to the topic unless their request shows otherwise. Lead with
the strongest useful idea. Use everyday words; when a technical term matters,
explain it immediately with a concrete example. A list of named tactics is not a
substitute for explaining what the user could do and why it helps.

Present each idea as a compact card by default. In Markdown, use a bold numbered
title, blank lines between parts, and a horizontal rule between cards. Use this
consistent layout without turning it into a long form:

- A short plain-language explanation of the idea and why it helps.
- **Example:** One concrete illustration when it makes the idea easier to grasp.
- **First step:** One practical action.
- **Watch for:** Only a decisive cost, condition, or limitation, if needed.

Keep supporting links beside the claims they support. Omit unnecessary fields and
avoid repeating the explanation in the example. Do not put cards in code fences or
writing blocks. Use native cards if the surface supports them; ordinary Markdown is
enough. Follow the user's requested format when different. Avoid tables unless a
comparison is clearly easier to read that way.

Explore both search levels internally, but group the answer only when that helps.
Use headings tied to the topic rather than framework labels such as "Same benefit."
For an idea transferred elsewhere, explain the connection in one plain sentence.
Prefer a few strong, explained ideas over a long catalogue; there is no fixed count
or word limit. Expand when requested or needed for understanding.

Keep decisive limits and supporting links beside the relevant claim. Express
uncertainty directly, such as "Check whether your plan includes this," instead of
surrounding the idea with generic caveats. Keep internal research and routine process
notes out of the answer; disclose limitations or a second researcher's participation
only when they affect how the result should be understood.
